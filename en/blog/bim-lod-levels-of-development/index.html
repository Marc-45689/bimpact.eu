BIM LOD: what each level really requires

2026-10-10 · 5 min read

"Model to LOD 300." The line shows up in almost every set of BIM requirements. On its own, it guarantees nothing. An LOD only protects the project when it is defined object by object, phase by phase, and read the same way by everyone involved.

What is LOD in BIM?

LOD (level of development) measures two things at once: the geometric precision of an object and the amount of information it carries. It is often written as LOD = LoD + LoI.

  • LoD, level of detail: the geometry, from a generic volume to the exact shape of the installed product.
  • LoI, level of information: data you cannot see on screen, such as the product reference, power rating, manufacturer or maintenance interval.

The two rarely move at the same pace. A roughly drawn door can hold every piece of data the facility manager needs. A highly detailed cable tray can have no parameter filled in at all. And since a model has no drawing scale, with every object built at full scale, the real question is never "how much to draw". It is "which information, for which use, at which point".

LOD 100 to 500: the reference frameworks

Most projects rely on two overlapping classifications: the UK scale from PAS 1192-2 (LOD 1 to 7) and the US scale from AIA G202-2013 (LOD 100 to 500).

The BIMForum adds a step that building services teams know well: LOD 350. It covers the interfaces between systems, such as supports, openings and connections. It is often what makes clash detection genuinely usable in coordination.

My view: day to day, the "level of information need" introduced by EN 17412-1 (2020) is more useful than an LOD number. It forces you to write down, for each object and each use, the geometry, data and documents expected. PAS 1192 has since been superseded by the ISO 19650 series.

Why set the LOD per object rather than per project?

Because a single LOD is expensive and adds nothing. During design, a concrete slab at LOD 200 is often enough. A glazed facade or a plant room will need LOD 300 at the same stage. Both normally live in the same model.

For electrical and low-voltage trades, these are the levels I recommend:

  • Cable trays and ducts: real dimensions from technical design onward. They drive builder's work openings and coordination with HVAC and plumbing.
  • Light fixtures and devices: generic families during design, but already carrying power, voltage and circuit.
  • Distribution boards: footprint and clearance zone modeled early. The manufacturer reference can wait for construction.
  • As-built: simplified geometry, complete information for the CMMS.

The last point often surprises people. An as-built model can stay at LoD 200 with LoI 500. The facility manager needs the data, not the rendering.

Who sets the LOD, and where is it written?

The client states the need, the BIM manager translates it. The BIM requirements say what the model will be used for, during construction and operation. The BIM execution plan turns that need into levels per object. The BIM manager does not decide alone: they spell out what each LOD means and arbitrate when a coordinator asks for more detail than the contract requires.

The most effective tool is still a descriptive table written for each project. For each trade and object type (concrete column, cable tray, light fixture), it gives the geometric LoD per phase and the expected LoI, with the list of parameters. A reference image per phase is optional, but it prevents a lot of misunderstandings.

Four common mistakes

  • Over-modeling. LOD 400 required everywhere bloats files and slows the whole team down, with no benefit for the current phase.
  • Confusing detail with information. Good-looking geometry without parameters serves neither quantity take-off nor operation.
  • Ignoring phase transitions. When moving from design to construction, contractors are not all appointed at once. LoD and LoI consistency has to be checked one by one.
  • Leaving the LOD implicit. Without a table, everyone reads "LOD 300" their own way. The disagreement surfaces at handover, at the worst possible moment.

Where to start

Open your next set of BIM requirements and look for the "LOD 300" line. If no object-by-object table comes with it, that is the first document to ask for, or to propose yourself. A few hours of scoping always cost less than reworking a model at handover.

Frequently asked questions

LOD stands for Level of Development. It describes both the geometric precision of an object (LoD, level of detail) and the amount of information attached to it (LoI, level of information).

LoD measures the graphic detail of the modeled object. LoI covers non-visible data: references, technical characteristics, maintenance information. LOD combines both.

At technical design and tender stage, LOD 300 is generally required: accurate 3D objects matching the specification, which contractors can use to price the job. Some objects can stay at LOD 200 if their use does not justify more.

The client states their needs in the BIM requirements. The BIM manager translates them into levels of development per object and per phase in the BIM execution plan, together with each trade's coordinator.
