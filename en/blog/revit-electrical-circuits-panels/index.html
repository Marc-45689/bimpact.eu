Electrical design in Revit: circuits, panels, schedules

2026-10-10 · 4 min read

In Revit, a socket placed on a plan is just a symbol until it is connected to something. Linked to a circuit, itself tied to a panel, it feeds load summaries that update on their own. Here is the method I recommend for an electrical package, from the architect's model to the schedules.

1. Set up a template for the local network

The templates shipped with Revit are often set for North American networks (120/208 V, 480 V). Start with Manage > MEP Settings > Electrical Settings and declare the project's distribution systems: in France, 230 V single-phase and 400 V three-phase. Then save everything into your own template, with your families, tags and schedule templates. That setting saves time on every project that follows.

2. Link the architect's model without copying it

The electrical model is a separate file. The architect's model comes in as a Revit link, or an IFC link, positioned origin to origin. To place devices, there are two options:

  • Face-based families, which attach directly to the walls and ceilings of the link. This is the option to favor: nothing is duplicated.
  • Copy/Monitor a few elements from the link (walls, ceilings, levels), when a family needs a native host or to create spaces. Keep it to the strict minimum.

If you copy, start from a purged copy of the architect's model. Only the types actually used will be transferred.

3. Place panels and create circuits

Panels are electrical equipment families. Once placed, assign them the right distribution system. From then on the logic never changes: select the devices to supply, create a power circuit, pick the source panel. To add or remove a device, edit the circuit.

A device set to the wrong voltage will be refused by Revit. And while wires can be drawn on plan, it is the circuits, not the wires, that carry the information.

4. Handle lighting with switch systems

In a reflected ceiling plan view, place the fixtures, then the switches. Fixtures join a lighting circuit connected to the panel, and a switch system records what controls what. The model can then answer concrete questions: which fixtures this switch turns off, which load this circuit carries.

5. Use panel schedules

The panel schedule lists circuits, their loads and their distribution per phase. Duplicate the schedule template and adapt it to your usual documents, with loads shown per phase to check balancing. Change the power of a heater type and the schedule follows immediately. Wire and cable tray lengths can be quantified the same way.

6. Model cable trays for coordination

For the other trades, cable trays and conduits are often what matters most, because they are what crosses ductwork and pipework. Model them at their real size and mounting heights from the start of coordination. Before each exchange, check their IFC class on export.

What Revit does not do: regulatory sizing. Cable sizes, voltage drops and protective devices under the applicable wiring rules (NF C 15-100 in France) are calculated in dedicated electrical design software. Revit holds the information model and load summaries, the design software holds the calculation report. They complement each other; neither replaces the other. Personally, I pair Caneco with its Caneco BIM plug-in for Revit. On one project, a subcontractor actually supplied their electrical schematics using this method.

Where to start

With the template. A template set for the local network, with your families and schedules, pays for itself by the second project. Once it is in place, creating circuits and producing a load summary per panel becomes routine, and your time goes to coordination, where it counts.
