BIM explained: more than a 3D model

2026-09-15 · 5 min read

BIM (Building Information Modeling) is becoming increasingly common in the construction industry. But behind the acronym lies a persistent misconception: many still think BIM is just about 3D. That misses the point entirely.

BIM isn't (just) 3D

If the "M" in BIM evokes the digital model, it's really the "I", for Information, that forms its true core. BIM relies on one or more three-dimensional digital models, but its value comes from the data they hold and the collaborative work they enable.

In practice, BIM changes the very nature of technical drawing:

  • The digital model: a 3D graphical representation of the building, designed as an information carrier rather than a simple visual.
  • From drawing to object: a wall, a door or a window are no longer just lines on a plan, they are objects carrying physical and functional properties (materials, dimensions, performance, etc.).
  • Collaboration: every project stakeholder enriches and shares data within the same model, which streamlines exchanges between trades.

In other words, BIM is above all a living database, continuously updated by every professional involved, from the programming phase through to the building's eventual deconstruction.

The dimensions of BIM: 3D, 4D, 5D...

BIM comes in several levels, each progressively enriching the digital model:

  • 3D: the three-dimensional digital model of the building.
  • 4D: adding the time dimension (construction phasing, execution schedule).
  • 5D: integrating the material and human resources needed for the site.

Each additional dimension turns the model into an increasingly complete project management tool.

Why adopt BIM? The concrete benefits

Technical benefits

  • Better visualization: 3D makes the overall building easier to understand for everyone involved.
  • Stronger collaboration: information flows more smoothly between project stakeholders.
  • Real time savings: no more redundant re-entry, many tasks become automated.
  • Better interface management: conflicts between trades (for example between plumbing and electrical) are caught much earlier.
  • Project optimization: greater adaptability and faster calculations.
  • Higher quality: better-controlled design, and therefore a more reliable finished building.

In my CFO-CFA technical studies work, this is one of the benefits I see most directly on site: interference between an electrical cable tray and a ventilation duct, for example, surfaces at the coordination model stage rather than during installation.

Business and organizational benefits

  • A communication tool: BIM makes exchanges with clients easier thanks to clear visual media.
  • High responsiveness: changes are quick to make and their impact is immediately measurable.
  • An asset for recruiting: a tool that appeals to the younger generation of construction professionals.
  • Higher profitability: a streamlined organization that translates into productivity gains.

In summary

BIM isn't a simple 3D visualization gadget: it's a collaborative way of working, structured around a shared database, that follows the building throughout its entire life cycle. For construction professionals, it means time saved, a mark of quality and a genuine competitive edge.
