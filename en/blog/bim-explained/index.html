What is BIM? Much more than a 3D model

2026-09-15 · 3 min read

Many building professionals still sum up BIM as "3D". That misses the point. BIM is first of all a way to produce, share and maintain the information of a building, from the brief to demolition.

What is BIM?

BIM (Building Information Modeling) refers both to one or more 3D digital models and to all the data attached to them. The "M" evokes the model. The heart of it is the "I": information.

On a 2D drawing, a wall is a line. In a BIM model, it is an object that knows its material, thickness, fire rating and thermal performance. Each party enriches these objects in a shared model instead of redrawing everything on their own.

A Revit training course (HPSoft) gives a telling example. Without a shared model, changing the diameter of a drainage pipe meant re-entering the information about seven times: general plan, detail drawings, schedule, cost estimate, pressure-loss calculations. With BIM, you change it once and everything else follows.

BIM is therefore less a piece of software than a living database, updated by every party throughout the life of the building.

The dimensions of BIM: from 3D to 7D

Each dimension adds a layer of information to the model:

  • 3D: the building geometry, which makes interfaces between trades readable.
  • 4D: time, linking the model to the schedule to follow construction phasing.
  • 5D: costs and the resources the site needs.
  • 6D and 7D: sustainability, then building operation and maintenance.

Not every project uses all of them. It depends on what the client wants to do with the model, and that is exactly what should be written down from the start.

What BIM changes in practice

On the technical side, the most visible gain is early clash detection. When all discipline models are overlaid, a network crossing another one shows up on screen long before construction. Add to that the end of re-keying, a better understanding of the building for everyone involved, and changes whose impact is measured immediately.

In my work on electrical and low-voltage packages, this is the benefit I see most directly: a clash between a cable tray and a ventilation duct shows up in the coordination model, not when the installers are on site.

For a company, BIM is also a communication tool: a client grasps a model faster than a set of drawings. It also attracts young professionals trained on these tools, which matters in a sector that is hiring.

BIM is not bought, it is organized

A Revit licence is not enough to do BIM. The benefit comes from the organization around the model: written goals, a BIM execution plan shared by everyone, levels of development defined object by object. That is where I advise starting, even before choosing the tools.
